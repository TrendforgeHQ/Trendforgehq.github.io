## The policy shift
Tidal announced that it will delete any music that distributors confirm was created solely with artificial‑intelligence tools. The decision follows the service’s earlier practice of labeling AI‑generated tracks and cutting royalty payments for those releases. The new rule applies only to songs produced entirely by generative tools; recordings that involve human input may stay on the catalog.

## How the ban is enforced
Tidal has partnered with Ircam Amplify, a French AI music‑detection company, to help identify fully AI‑generated works. The partnership was highlighted in a post on X, where the platform noted that 94 % of AI‑badged tracks it had flagged came from the producer Suno. The company also said that 43 % of the songs uploaded in the past 90 days were marked as wholly AI‑generated.

## Context in the industry
This summer, a coalition of record labels proposed a new system for tagging AI‑generated songs on streaming services. While none of the labels’ exact proposal has been adopted, several platforms—Tidal, Spotify, Deezer and Apple Music—have built their own tagging mechanisms. Spotify now labels some AI‑generated artist identities with an AI Persona badge and allows artists to opt in for review before releases appear.

## Why the move matters
By banning AI‑only songs, Tidal becomes the first digital streaming service to take an absolute stance against fully machine‑generated music. The change removes a category of tracks that could compete with human artists for streams and royalties. As Tidal’s leadership noted, the platform wants to protect creators from songs that might otherwise be buried under AI‑generated noise or copied by impostors.

## Numbers and expectations
In July, Tidal began labeling tracks as wholly AI‑generated and cutting royalties. The service estimates that about 29 million tracks could be removed by the start of 2027 if the policy remains in place. The platform also reported that 96 % of the AI songs it has already flagged have received zero streams in the last 90 days.

## Limitations and uncertainty
The ban applies only to music that distributors say was created solely with AI tools. It does not address music where AI is used as a supportive tool in a broader human production process. The policy also relies on external detection tools, and the accuracy of those tools has not been independently verified. SoundCloud, for instance, recently updated its policy to tackle harmful side effects of AI music such as spam and impersonation, but it has not adopted a similar ban.

## What to watch next
Industry observers will likely monitor how the ban affects upload traffic and whether other services follow Tidal’s lead. Spotify’s current badge system and Apple Music’s handling of AI‑generated content provide possible models for alternative approaches. The broader debate over how to classify and regulate machine‑made music will continue, especially as detection technologies improve and more artists experiment with AI tools.
