## The Safety Gap
Microsoft’s president Brad Smith has publicly compared the regulatory oversight of artificial intelligence to that of grocery‑store milk. He says AI has fewer safety rules than common household items such as milk and washing machines, and that the gap creates a risk for autonomous agents, cybersecurity threats and corporate accountability.

## Calls for Concrete Safeguards
During a BBC interview, Smith urged the creation of legal accountability for rogue AI agents, and called for a “kill switch” that would allow a human to pause or shut down an AI system mid‑task. The idea echoes a request made by Microsoft chief executive Satya Nadella, who also said AI models should carry an “emergency brake.”

## Industry Response and Sign‑Up Challenges
At a recent summit, executives from OpenAI, Anthropic, Nvidia, Meta, Google and Elon Musk’s X signed a one‑page, morally binding accord. The agreement requires independent auditors and safeguards to keep models from going rogue. Microsoft did not sign the accord, a fact noted by The Telegraph. Smith welcomed the accord and said Microsoft would be willing to sign it, but he added that the agreement is no longer sufficient to address the biggest risks.

## Voluntary Measures and Their Limits
Smith highlighted the AI Security Institute, launched in 2024 under the UK government, which has secured voluntary agreements that let it test frontier models before they ship. He warned that voluntary commitments may do little to curb the largest dangers because companies that already prioritize safety are more likely to comply, while those that pose the greatest risk can ignore them.

## What Readers Should Watch
Brad Smith, Microsoft’s president, has told the BBC that AI is currently working with fewer safety rules than commodities like milk or washing machines, and this should push governments, with the UK out front, to write binding controls, including kill switches and mandatory reporting when AI agents misbehave. Watch for policy proposals that may introduce mandatory reporting, kill switches and binding controls, as well as the outcome of Microsoft’s decision on the industry accord. The conversation also signals that even large corporations recognize the need for tighter safety nets around autonomous AI.

## Key Takeaway
The evidence shows that Microsoft’s leadership acknowledges a safety shortfall in AI regulation compared to everyday products, and is pushing for emergency‑stop mechanisms and stronger oversight. However, voluntary agreements alone may not fully contain the risks, underscoring the need for clearer, enforceable rules.
