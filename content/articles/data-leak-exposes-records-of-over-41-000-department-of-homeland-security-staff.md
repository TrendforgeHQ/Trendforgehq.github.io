## Data Breach Exposes Tens of Thousands of Federal Records

A cyber incident has exposed the personal information of more than 41,000 employees working for the U.S. Department of Homeland Security (DHS). The leak represents data equivalent to roughly one-fifth of the agency's total workforce, which consists of approximately 220,000 staff members. The published database affects personnel across multiple critical divisions, including U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) officers at ports of entry and airports, as well as Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) personnel involved in federal enforcement actions.

The exposed records were part of a data breach. Furthermore, the breach impacts investigative roles; ICE accounts for roughly 10,000 of the exposed records, with about one-third of those coming from Homeland Security Investigations, the dedicated investigative wing of ICE. The leak also revealed information linked to undercover investigators and border security agents.

## Source of the Hack and Distribution Channels

The compromised material was shared with journalist maia arson crimew, who then provided it to DDoSecrets. Distributed Denial of Secrets (DDoSecrets), a nonprofit organization that hosts leaked datasets in the public interest, published the information online. The collective stated that it received the stolen dataset from a hacktivist entity referring to itself as the Department of Peace or the Ministry of Peace.

The group responsible created a specialized search tool called Icepick to allow users to perform detailed searches of employee names, offices, and work assignments. Before reaching DDoSecrets, the compromised DHS data was initially provided to journalist and hacker maia arson crimew. Outside analysis and verification confirmed the validity of the dataset; independent reporting verified a portion of the published records as authentic by comparing the names of known DHS employees against an offline copy of the leaked file.

## System Impact, Tech Vendor Links, and Government Response

The leak highlights vulnerabilities surrounding identity management and internal operational logging tools. The compromised Okta records contained IT and log-in details connected to user accounts, specifically revealing information regarding internal applications and systems that individual employees were authorized to access. In addition, references in the leak cite artificial intelligence tools used within federal workflows, including OpenAI’s ChatGPT, Google’s Gemini, and Microsoft’s Copilot. Records also mention Penlink, an entity that has held a vendor contract with ICE for years.

In response to the incident, a spokesperson for Homeland Security confirmed that DHS is aware of a potential breach and is actively working to address the situation swiftly and effectively. Meanwhile, figures named in the leak, including Noem, McCord, Hegseth, and Krachenfels, did not respond to requests for comment, and the hacker responsible did not respond to inquiries regarding how the breach was executed.
