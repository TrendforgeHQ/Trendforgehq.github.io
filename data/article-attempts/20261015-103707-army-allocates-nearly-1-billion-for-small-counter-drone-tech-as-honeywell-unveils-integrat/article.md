## Rising Counter‑Drone Spending Reflects Battlefield Reality
The Defense Department’s fiscal‑2027 budget request earmarks almost $1 billion for Army small counter‑unmanned aerial system (cUAS) procurement. That amount is nearly twice the funding granted in the enacted FY26 budget, signalling a sharpened focus on counter‑drone capabilities. The allocation covers a range of items, including batteries, expeditionary mobile platforms, sensors, and logistics support for small cUAS. It also funds dismounted, handheld, and wearable systems designed to protect against Group 1 and Group 2 UAS threats.
The uptick comes amid reports that inexpensive first‑person‑view drones have struck multi‑million‑dollar tanks on modern battlefields, such as the Ukraine‑Russia conflict.

## Honeywell’s Integrated Counter‑Drone Software
Honeywell Aerospace’s subsidiary, EMS Defense Technologies, has developed software that unites data from up to seven different sensors—radar, cameras, radio‑frequency detectors, acoustic sensors and more—into a single operator interface. Electro‑optical and infrared cameras let users examine objects closely, while acoustic sensors help identify drones by sound. The system is designed for operational readiness rather than experimental use; Honeywell has demonstrated the software to U.S. government and military representatives, as well as local law enforcement and state and city officials.
At the Association of the United States Army’s annual meeting, Honeywell displayed a mockup of the mobile configuration mounted on a Ford F‑150. The effort builds on more than a year of joint development and systems‑integration work.

## What This Means for Soldiers and Agencies
The Army’s increased funding will support a broader mix of small cUAS platforms, from handheld units to expeditionary mobile systems. By integrating multiple sensor feeds, Honeywell’s software could streamline the detection and neutralization process for soldiers on the ground and for civilian agencies dealing with drone incursions. The combination of diverse sensors—radar, visual, RF and acoustic—provides a more comprehensive picture of the aerial threat environment, potentially reducing response time and improving accuracy.

## Limitations and Uncertainties
While the budget request outlines a significant investment, specific procurement timelines and procurement quantities remain undefined. Honeywell’s software, though demonstrated, is still in a developmental phase; its operational readiness and field performance have not yet been fully validated in combat scenarios. The technology’s effectiveness against emerging drone tactics, such as swarming or low‑observable platforms, is not yet established.

## Watch for Integration and Deployment
Readers should monitor how the Army translates the new funding into actual acquisitions and whether Honeywell’s software is adopted as part of the broader counter‑drone strategy. Key indicators include contract awards, field trials, and reports from deployed units on the usability and reliability of integrated sensor systems. As drone threats evolve, the ability to fuse multiple detection modalities into a single, operator‑friendly interface will likely become increasingly critical for both military and civilian stakeholders.
