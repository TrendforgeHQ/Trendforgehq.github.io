## The Core Tension Over Electricity

Recent observations in Ashburn, Virginia—a key hub known as Data Center Alley—highlight a fundamental issue facing the technology sector. The friction surrounding data centers centers heavily on electricity rather than public relations framing. While the tech industry can work to improve its public image, it remains bound to the physical consequences of its massive power consumption.

At the same time, views on the driver of community pushback vary. One perspective contends that the backlash is not fundamentally about electricity or water usage, pointing out that research suggests data centers can actually help lower electricity prices as higher demand drives increased energy production. According to calculations by economic analyst James Pethokoukis, even if data center water consumption quadruples by 2030, it would still represent just 1% of the total supply, noting that far more water could be saved through other conservation measures than by shutting down every data center. Nevertheless, public concern remains visible; on July 18, a national day of protest against data centers involved 142 protests spanning 42 states, driven in part by the fact that these facilities consume massive amounts of electricity.

## Off-Grid Power Solutions and Massive Scale

Most AI data centers being constructed across the country operate differently from traditional facilities tied directly to local grids. Because the scale of power required is so vast, companies are increasingly establishing facilities in rural locations that lack existing grid infrastructure. To function in these areas, developers must supply their own "behind the meter" power, relying primarily on natural-gas-powered turbines alongside diesel generators for backup energy.

The scale required for modern AI workloads is substantial. The smallest data centers considered attractive for AI training require at least 300 megawatts, a load exceeding the power draw of the Denver International Airport. To meet these requirements independently, major technology firms are making unprecedented financial commitments. For instance, Anthropic has reportedly agreed to pay $3.4 billion for gas turbines, marking what is described as the largest power generation purchase by an AI company.

## Community Impact and Shifting Policy

While off-grid self-generation might initially appear to protect public grids from strain, generating electricity at this magnitude creates localized environmental and social effects. Historically, when public utilities constructed infrastructure of this scale, local communities expected a direct say in site selection and environmental impact. Today, private technology companies are executing energy projects of identical magnitude, prompting growing demands for public oversight and usage transparency.

Policy responses to this shift are taking shape at multiple government levels. In Texas, Governor Greg Abbott transitioned from participating in ribbon-cutting ceremonies for major data centers to issuing explicit demands regarding their power usage, site selection, and overall economic contributions. Policymakers and government entities are increasingly requiring data center operators to generate their own power or even provide a net increase in total grid capacity.

## Global Expansion and Local Constraints

Even when public electrical grids are nearby, they are frequently unable to supply the massive volume of power required by modern facilities. As a result, companies are looking beyond domestic borders to satisfy their infrastructure needs. For example, Google announced the day after the recent U.S. elections that it will invest $15 billion in data center projects in Malaysia.

Ultimately, whether tech companies build dedicated natural gas plants in rural regions or expand into international markets, physical energy constraints remain a defining operational challenge. Managing localized community impact, ensuring transparent resource usage, and balancing private energy generation with public expectations will dictate how the industry navigates its expanding footprint.

## What the Evidence Shows

- Data center operations demand immense power, with the smallest AI training facilities drawing 300 megawatts—more than the Denver International Airport.
- To bypass limited grid infrastructure, companies are buying gas turbines and diesel backup generators for rural sites, including Anthropic's reported $3.4 billion turbine commitment.
- Government officials are pushing for usage transparency and off-grid self-generation, while tech firms also expand internationally, such as Google's $15 billion investment in Malaysia.
